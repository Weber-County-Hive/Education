How a Book Leaves Every Utah School, in Plain Words

The rule. Since 2024, Utah has a law called H.B. 29. If three school districts take the same book out of their schools because it breaks the state’s rule on sexual content, it comes out of every public school in the state. (Two districts plus five charter schools count too.) Only the State School Board can vote to bring it back.

How many. By Sept. 28, 2026, 44 books had been taken out statewide. Every one started with school districts, not charter schools. Davis School District is on 43 of the 44. Weber, Ogden and Box Elder school districts are on none.

Who asks for it. Anyone in a school community can file a challenge: parents, students, school workers and school board members. Brooke Stephens, a Davis County mom with a group called Utah Parents United, has filed many challenges and runs book-rating websites. She spoke at the Legislature in favor of H.B. 29.

Her giving. State records show she gave small amounts, $359.05 in all from 2023 to 2026, to eight candidates. They include $50 to Rep. Ken Ivory, who wrote H.B. 29, and money to four people running for the State School Board. One of them, Rod Hall, won his seat.

What the State School Board did. On Aug. 6, 2026, the board voted to spend $60,000 on a review of how books get into school libraries. It voted 10 to 5 to add Board Member Emily Green’s list of survey questions. Rod Hall was one of the 10 yes votes. Green says the board needs to understand how book sellers reach schools. Districts had until Oct. 16 to choose whether to take part.

Who gives the board money. Board members’ campaign reports show the five who voted no on Green’s questions all got money from a group called Education First Utah. Carolyn Sharette, who leads the company that runs Utah’s biggest group of charter schools, gave to six members; five of them voted yes. Money given does not prove why anyone voted. See the full table.

Charter schools. Charter schools are free public schools with their own boards. State reports name no charter school in any statewide removal, and state auditors did not check charter schools.

How the $60,000 got approved. Two board committees voted yes first (June 5 and July 14), then the full board on Aug. 6. The board’s plan runs 26 weeks and starts with 12 school districts and 3 charter schools.

What a tip showed. Emails that came in with a tip show Green asking the board’s auditor whether the review could be a formal audit, a paragraph taken out of her memo before the meeting, one member saying schools would be paid to take part, and another saying the questions should be optional. See what the emails say.

How one book list moved. RatedBooks.org is a nonprofit Brooke Stephens leads; it rates books by how much sexual content they have. State auditors used its list, which they say came from “a concerned legislator” they don’t name. Emails from a tip show Rep. Ken Ivory asking a Jordan School District board member to challenge Bag of Bones, which a lawmaker can’t do himself, and a survey draft from Mike Green for Board Member Emily Green that names RatedBooks.org. Green’s board biography says her husband is Mike. See each step and its record.

Other states. Most states remove books one district at a time. Only Utah and South Carolina take books out of every school at once; Tennessee has a law that could, but hasn’t used it. Utah has 44 books removed statewide, South Carolina 22. In both, a few people filed most of the challenges. See how they compare.

What would help. A public list showing each school, date and book; each district’s challenge counts; the name of whoever made the 186-book list auditors used; and charter schools checked too. See all ten ideas and questions you can ask.

Our grade for that board vote: C−. It got a D for transparency: the extra questions and memo were added at the meeting and we didn’t find them on the public notice, the board says no survey questions have been shared publicly, and the copy of the minutes on the state’s public notice site is still an unapproved version with a counting error. A C means it meets what is required, and nothing more. The law itself, H.B. 29, gets a C−, because one district’s decision can now reach every other district.
