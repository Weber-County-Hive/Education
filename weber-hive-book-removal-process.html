How a Book Gets Pulled From Every Utah School

How one rule can take a book out of every public school in Utah, what the State School Board voted to do about library books, and who gives money to the people making these choices. All from public records.

Posted Oct. 31, 2026 · Updated Nov. 1, 2026

New to this? Read it in plain words About 600 words.
Share on Facebook
Short version
  • The rule: since 2024, a Utah law called H.B. 29 says that when three school districts take out the same book because it breaks the state’s rule on sexual content (“objective sensitive material”), it comes out of every public school in Utah. Two districts plus five charter schools also count. Only the State School Board can vote to put it back (KSL).
  • How many: by Sept. 28, 2026, 44 books had been taken out statewide. School districts started every one; no charter school did. Davis School District helped remove 43 of them. Weber, Ogden and Box Elder districts helped remove none (KUER; TownLift).
  • A parent leader: Brooke Stephens, a Davis County parent with Utah Parents United, spoke in favor of H.B. 29 at the Legislature (Deseret News). State campaign records list small gifts from a Brooke Stephens of Layton, $359.05 in all, to eight candidates from 2023 to 2026. They include the bill’s sponsor and four people running for the State School Board (disclosures.utah.gov).
  • The board’s vote: on Aug. 6, 2026, the State School Board agreed to spend $60,000 studying how books get into school libraries. It voted 10–5 to add Board Member Emily Green’s extra list of questions, called an addendum (USBE minutes). Districts don’t have to take part; they had until Oct. 16, 2026 to say yes.
  • One list, step by step: RatedBooks.org, a nonprofit Stephens leads, rates books. State auditors used “a list provided by a concerned legislator” from ratedbooks.org. A tip shows Rep. Ivory asking a Jordan board member to file a challenge, and a survey draft for Board Member Green that names RatedBooks.org. See How one book list moved.
  • Who funds the board: state records show the board members who voted no on Green’s addendum all got money from the group Education First Utah. Most of the money tied to charter schools went to members who voted yes. See Who gives money to the board. Money given does not show why anyone voted the way they did.

In plain words: someone asks the school to take a book out. If the book clearly breaks the state’s rule, district leaders can take it out on their own. If it’s a judgment call, a group of parents and staff (a review committee) decides. Once enough districts take the same book out, it leaves every public school in Utah.

Built from KSL’s description of H.B. 29 and the bill’s own summary (KSL; H.B. 29 bill page). Every bill behind this, with votes and grades, is on the Bill Tracker: Utah’s School Book Laws, 2022–2026.

Which books, and which districts

As of Sept. 28, 2026, 44 titles are on the statewide list. Every one was triggered by school districts; no charter school appears on any title. Davis School District is on 43 of the 44. Weber School District, Ogden School District and Box Elder School District do not appear on any title.

What about charter schools? Charter schools are public schools run by their own boards instead of a school district. The state’s yearly reports to lawmakers credit every statewide removal to school districts and name no charter school (USBE 2024 report; USBE 2025 report). State auditors visited 2 charter schools but said they did not review charter schools’ compliance, because the law only requires them to audit districts (state audit, Oct. 2025). A charter school can still take a book off its own shelves; that would not show up on any public list unless it counted toward a statewide removal.

How Utah compares to other states

Most states remove books one district at a time. PEN America, a group that tracks and opposes book bans, counted 11,982 school book bans nationwide from July 2025 to June 2026. Texas had 8,193 and Florida 3,212, district by district (Texas Public Radio; FOX 9).

Only a few states take a book out of every school at once:

StateHow it worksBooks removed statewide
Utah (H.B. 29, 2024)When 3 districts, or 2 districts plus 5 charter schools, remove the same book as clearly over the line, it leaves every public school. The State School Board can vote to put it back.44 as of Sept. 28, 2026; none added in October (The Globe)
South Carolina (State Board rule 43-170, 2024)A parent who disagrees with a district’s decision can appeal to the State Board, which votes on each book. A book with any sexual content comes out of every school, K–12.22 (21 removed, 1 restricted); none added since May 2025 (ACLU of South Carolina; SC Daily Gazette)
Tennessee (H.B. 843, 2024)A state commission can order a book out statewide.None so far (PEN America)

PEN America’s Utah page calls Utah and South Carolina “the only two states” with statewide bans (PEN America).

Utah: a few districts, and a few peopleKUTV reported 33 of the first 34 statewide removals were first removed in Davis School District. A parents’ group, Let Utah Read, found 24 of 25 challenges filed in Davis in the 2025 school year came from one person (KUTV). As of October 2026, only nine Utah districts had filed complaints that led to a statewide removal; Davis and Washington took part in 43 and 32 of the 44 (The Globe).
South Carolina: one parent, many challenges“One parent in Beaufort County has brought 14 of the challenges the state board has considered” (SC Daily Gazette, May 2025). PEN America says most of the books removed at the state level came from one woman who challenged 97 books in Beaufort County (PEN America).
NationallyA 2023 Washington Post review of more than 1,000 challenges found 11 people filed 60% of them (as reported by Book Riot).
What each side saysStephens told KUTV she does not remove books herself; review committees decide (KUTV). Her May 2026 newsletter says “the books themselves are the reason they are being removed,” “as agreed upon by multiple committees in multiple school districts” (a tip came in). The ACLU of South Carolina, which is suing over that state’s rule, says the state “is continuing to leave educational decisions for all students up to one parent” (WBTV).

The timeline plan grew out of a May 18, 2026 committee motion directing staff to bring back a timeline “for conducting a comprehensive review and audit” of school library practices (USBE action item). Two committees approved it before the full board: Standards and Assessment on June 5 (4–1 in that committee’s own summary, with Sarah Reale voting no; later documents call it unanimous) and Law and Licensing on July 14, unanimously (June 5 summary; July 14 summary).

What the review will look at

The plan is called the “School Library Materials Acquisition and Procurement Review.” Board documents describe three phases (KPCW/Salt Lake Tribune, Aug. 31, 2026):

  1. Money: a fiscal analysis of what schools spend on library materials.
  2. Supply chain: a technical review of how books are bought and the contracts behind them.
  3. Staff: a statewide look at library staff qualifications.

KUTV later reported the phases also cover regulatory steps and parent complaint logs, which show how districts respond when a parent challenges a book. The board’s library media specialist, Davina Sauthoff, said the aim is to understand the process “from the vendor perspective” (KUTV, Oct. 14, 2026).

What the board’s own timeline says. The plan staff posted runs 26 weeks in four phases: money (8 weeks), supply chain and buying (7 weeks), rules, training and complaint logs (7 weeks), and a final report (4 weeks). The first two phases use a sample of 12 school districts and 3 charter schools; the third covers every district and charter. It estimates 680 hours of state staff time and 789 hours of school district staff time. It does not mention paying districts for that time. Its first dates ran June 8 to Dec. 7, 2026; on Aug. 6 the board moved the start to Aug. 10 and shifted the rest to match (School Library Monitoring Timeline; USBE minutes). One line in the timeline lists 534 district hours for phase 3 but also describes it as about 3 hours across the whole state, so those two numbers don’t match.

Who signed up (board agenda for Nov. 6, 2026): superintendents got the sign-up form Sept. 30 and were asked to answer by Oct. 16. Superintendents in southwest Utah asked for more time, so the form was still open. As of the agenda, four districts (counting the Utah Schools for the Deaf and the Blind) and three charter schools had agreed to take part, out of 39 responses. The agenda also says “no official survey questions for the School Library Monitoring Review about purchasing or otherwise have been shared publicly,” and that Board Members Green and Brinton attended the southwest superintendents’ Oct. 14 board meeting about it. The Standards and Assessment Committee takes it up Nov. 6 (USBE Nov. 6, 2026 agenda).
Deadline: taking part is voluntary. Districts had until Oct. 16, 2026 to decide whether to join the data-gathering and survey. No date has been set for the review to finish (KUTV, Oct. 14, 2026).

May 18: Green asks whether it can be a formal auditBefore the committee meeting, Green emailed staff the motion she planned to make. What would make this clearer for the public

These ideas come from gaps in the public record, not from either side of the book debate. They would help people who want more books removed and people who want fewer, because both would be able to see what is happening.

1. One public list with dates and schoolsThe state’s yearly reports list which districts removed each book (USBE 2025 report). One running public list showing every school, the date it acted, and the book, including any charter school, would let anyone check how the statewide rule was reached. Who could do it: the State School Board.
2. Challenge counts for every districtHow many challenges each district gets, and from how many different people, is not posted by the state. KUTV found most removals start in one district (KUTV). Who could do it: each district, or the Legislature by law.
3. Name who made the 186-title listState auditors worked from a 186-title list that, KSL reports, an unnamed lawmaker made and rated (KSL). Knowing who made a list the state relied on is basic public information. Who could do it: the Legislature or its auditors.
4. Check charter schools tooThe state audit did not review charter schools, because the law only requires district audits (state audit). Charter schools are public schools paid with tax money. Who could do it: the Legislature, by changing the audit law.
5. Post the final survey before it goes outThe board adopted the addendum’s memo “for staff refinement and committee discussion, not as a final form” (USBE minutes). Posting the final questions, and what the $60,000 pays for, before districts answer would let the public see what is being asked. Who could do it: the State School Board.
6. Post the approved minutes everywhereThe board approved its Aug. 6 minutes on Sept. 4, 2026, and the approved version on its own website counts every vote correctly (approved minutes). The copy on the state’s public notice site is still an unapproved version that lists one member on both sides of a vote and counts 16 votes on a 15-member board (notice-site copy). The public notice site is where many people look first. Who could do it: the State School Board, by posting the approved version there.
7. A clear path to a “put it back” voteOnly the State School Board can return a book to every school. We did not find a posted process or schedule for how a book gets that vote, or a record of one ever happening. Who could do it: the State School Board.
8. Post what each book bill costsA fiscal note is the Legislature’s estimate of what a bill costs. Five of the six book bills have not had theirs read for the Hive’s Bill Tracker yet, so their money grades are blank. Who can help: the Legislature posts them on each bill page; the Hive will add them.
9. Show what the state pays to defend the lawH.B. 29 has the state cover claims against people who follow it, and a federal lawsuit over the law is underway (Deseret News). A public total of what defending the law costs would answer the money question. Who could do it: the Attorney General’s Office or the Legislature.
10. Say whether districts get paidAn email that came in with a tip has one member saying schools in the study “will be compensated.” The board’s posted timeline counts 789 hours of district staff time but says nothing about payment (timeline). Who could do it: the State School Board.
